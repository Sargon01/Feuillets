## Principles

1. **Text before system.** Write before configuring.
2. **Plan without locking yourself in.** An outline is a hypothesis, not a contract.
3. **Structure serves writing**, never the reverse.
4. **Information appears when useful**, not permanently in front of you.
5. **Measure what helps**, not everything that can be measured.
6. **Rewrite without losing.** Versions should make experimentation safer.
7. **No imposed writing method.** Gardener, architect, or both.
8. **A text can become a project** even if it was not created as one.

> Start writing. Feuillets will still be there when your texts need to become a project.

The aim is the simplicity of a notebook, with a complete writing studio that appears only when the author needs it.

---

## Obsidian provides the environment; Feuillets provides the studio

Feuillets is not meant to become a separate application hidden inside Obsidian.

Obsidian provides:

- the vault;
- Markdown;
- links and backlinks;
- the editor;
- Canvas;
- sync chosen by the user;
- the plugin ecosystem.

Feuillets adds what long-form writers need specifically:

- a representation of the manuscript;
- structural operations;
- a Research bible;
- working Context;
- narrative views;
- a rewriting chain;
- coherent composition;
- editorial tools.

This division matters. Feuillets should not rebuild everything Obsidian already does well.

## A tool born from writing, not from a theory

Feuillets did not begin as an attempt to reproduce Scrivener inside Obsidian, nor as a checklist of features found in writing applications. It grew from the much more concrete experience of writing books.

After using word processors, specialist applications such as Scrivener or Ulysses, and systems assembled inside Obsidian, the same problem keeps returning: **writing software often asks too much from the writer.**

Some tools are powerful but complex. Others are elegant but become limiting as a manuscript grows. Many specialist systems encourage the author to model the book before writing it: character sheets, places, arcs, timelines, beats, subplots, databases, tables and properties.

Inside Obsidian, the opposite problem can appear: almost any writing environment can be built, but only after assembling plugins, templates, properties, queries and conventions. The writer ends up designing a system before being allowed to simply write.

Feuillets should reduce that friction rather than add another layer of it.

## The notebook and the pen

The most accurate image for Feuillets is probably not a cockpit, dashboard or narrative database.

**It is a notebook and a pen.**

A notebook does not ask for the genre, cast, story structure or target completion date before accepting one sentence. You open it and write.

A sentence. A list. An image. A line of dialogue. A scene that may never find a place. A beginning. A memory. A research note. A paragraph tried and abandoned.

Feuillets should preserve that basic freedom. The writer should never have to "prepare the software" before beginning the text.

---

## The project can come after the writing

This is one of the most important consequences of supporting free and existing-folder projects.

A Feuillets project does not have to be the starting point of the work. **It can be the result of the work beginning to take shape.**

Someone can start in Obsidian with nothing more than:

```text
My idea/
    A few ideas.md
    Possible opening.md
    Person met on the train.md
    Scene list.md
    Dialogue attempt.md
```

And somewhere nearby:

```text
Research/
    Historical notes.md
    Interesting article.md
    Photographs/
    Sources.md
```

For days or months, that can remain exactly as it is. No mandatory Binder structure. No project form. No required metadata.

Then the fragments begin to become something: an article, a short story, an essay, a series, a collection, a novel.

Only then does a simple action need to mean: **use this folder as a Feuillets project** or **initialize it as a structured Feuillets project**.

The author should not have to start over, migrate everything into a proprietary database, or rename the files simply to gain writing tools.

## Write before structuring — without opposing writing and planning

This philosophy does not mean planning is useless.

Writers plan. Some plan a lot, some very little. They make lists, possible chapters, timelines, key stages, cards or highly detailed outlines.

The problem begins when software turns that plan into a contract.

> **An outline is a hypothesis about the future book. The real book appears while writing it.**

A scene reveals something the outline did not predict. A character becomes more important. A subplot disappears. Two chapters need to merge. A planned ending stops being the right one.

The writer returns to the outline, changes it, writes again, and the text shifts again.

That is normal.

Feuillets should therefore support planning strongly while making it cheap to change your mind.

---

## Writing means rewriting

A serious manuscript is not produced in one pass.

Writing includes:

- trying;
- cutting;
- moving;
- splitting;
- merging;
- rewriting;
- abandoning;
- restoring;
- comparing;
- returning to an older direction.

A versioning system is useful only when it gives the author enough confidence to take risks.

That is why snapshots, comparisons, backups and narrative versions have distinct purposes. They should protect the work without turning revision into source-control administration.

## The text is the center of gravity

The manuscript is not an accidental output of a database.

The text is the center of the project.

Research, timelines, labels, goals and views exist to help the author write and revise that text. They are not more "real" than the sheet itself.

Feuillets therefore keeps the native Obsidian editor at the center and treats Preview as a reading surface, not as a second editor with another source of truth.

## No mandatory encyclopedia

A fiction project may contain rich notes for every person, place, object and historical event.

It may also contain almost none.

Research should be available when the book needs it, not as homework imposed before chapter one.

The same principle applies to metadata. Status, labels, dates, threads, goals and properties can make the project richer, but they must remain optional.

A blank Markdown file must still be a valid place to begin.

---

## Structure tools must remain tools

Binder, Cards, Outline, Storyline, Timeline and Canvas are useful because they let the author look at the same manuscript from different angles.

They must not become parallel systems that require synchronization or produce competing truths.

One structure, several representations.

### Information should appear when it becomes useful

The Context section embodies this principle particularly well: it surfaces nearby Research and chronological information when the current passage makes it relevant.

The author does not need the whole project bible permanently open.

Good software should know when to stay quiet.

## From gardener to architect — and back

The familiar opposition between "gardener" and "architect" is useful only if it remains fluid.

A writer may discover ten scenes freely, then build a careful outline, then abandon part of it because the written scenes reveal a better direction.

Feuillets should support this movement.

The **Notebook** helps the gardener capture and rearrange possibilities. The **Binder** helps the architect give them a working order. The author can move between them as often as necessary.

No mode should lock the project into a writing doctrine.

## From short text to book

A good writing environment should not require the author to know the final scale of a text before writing it.

One sheet can remain:

- an article;
- a note;
- a short story;
- an essay fragment.

Several sheets can later become:

- a series;
- a collection;
- a chapter;
- a book.

Compilation at file, folder, selection and project scope is therefore not merely an export convenience. It allows the shape of the publication to emerge after the writing begins.

---

## The Notebook: capture before organizing

The Notebook is the place where structure can remain provisional.

Ideas can be captured as text cards, grouped, linked, split, merged and developed into Idea Trees before any real manuscript file is created.

When a structure becomes convincing, the author can materialize it gradually into the Binder.

The relationship is intentionally one-way at the moment of choice, not permanent synchronization.

The Notebook helps you think.

The Binder remains the real structure of the manuscript.

## From fragment to book, then from book to editor

The writing process does not end when the manuscript becomes readable.

The author also needs to:

- compose;
- export;
- send;
- receive corrections;
- decide which changes to accept;
- produce another editorial version.

The DOCX Revision workflow exists because the manuscript should remain Markdown even when editorial conversation happens in Word.

Specialized domains can stay in companion plugins: Grammalecte can provide linguistic correction, while Courrier can manage submissions and correspondence.

The core does not have to absorb every surrounding profession.

## What writers' needs show

Writers generally need less software management, not more.

They need to be able to:

- start quickly;
- find the right passage;
- understand the structure;
- move scenes safely;
- keep useful Research nearby;
- compare versions;
- see continuity problems;
- assemble the real book;
- export it;
- exchange revisions without abandoning their source manuscript.

A feature is valuable when it reduces friction in one of these tasks.

---

## Progressive power

Feuillets can contain many tools without presenting them all at once.

The essential path can remain:

> Binder → sheet → Focus Mode → Preview

Then, as the project grows:

- Notes become useful;
- Research becomes useful;
- Cards or Outline become useful;
- Timeline becomes useful;
- Journal becomes useful;
- Analysis and Proofreading become useful;
- DOCX Revision becomes useful;
- advanced export settings become useful.

Power is not the same as permanent interface density.

## What Feuillets must not become

Feuillets should not become:

- a mandatory narrative database;
- a form that must be completed before writing;
- a general PKM that competes with Obsidian;
- an autonomous application hidden inside Obsidian;
- a feature race with every commercial writing program;
- a system where metadata matters more than text;
- a maintenance project that consumes more energy than writing.

Before adding something, ask:

1. Does it help write?
2. Does it help reread?
3. Does it help find something?
4. Does it help reorganize or rewrite?
5. Does it help finish or transmit the text?

If its only justification is to produce more data, more visualization or more control, its usefulness should be questioned.

> **The number of possibilities in Feuillets must never become the number of things the author is required to understand.**

---

## Maintenance: stability before the feature race

Feuillets already provides a broad writing studio.

Maintenance should therefore prioritize, in this order:

1. stability;
2. consistency between views;
3. ergonomics;
4. small improvements that are genuinely useful to writers.

A general rearchitecture or a new feature is not an improvement by itself. It is worth doing only when it makes writing, rewriting or handing off the manuscript materially simpler.

**Software sophistication should come from the needs of the project, not from the maintenance burden of the tool.**

---

## A design centered on the writer

The most important difference is not that Feuillets has more features than another application. That would be an endless competition.

Its difference can be more fundamental:

> **Feuillets is designed from the writer's work, not from a computer model of writing.**

The software does not try to explain how to build a story. It does not try to turn the manuscript into data. It does not pretend a graph can decide whether a chapter works.

It provides a space where the author can write, change their mind, move, delete, restart, return to an earlier state and gradually discover the shape of the text.
